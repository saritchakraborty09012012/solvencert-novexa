Propositions and
their Converses
Consider the following statement:
Statement 1:   If two sides of a triangle are equal, then the angles opposite
the equal sides are equal  .
Such a statement that is either true or false is called a   proposition  .
Now consider the following proposition:
Statement 2:   If two angles of a triangle are equal, then the sides opposite
the equal angles have equal lengths  .
The first proposition is of the form ‘if X then Y’. The second is of the
form ‘if Y then X’. We say that the second proposition is the   converse   of
the first proposition.
The statement ‘if X then Y’ is also written as ‘X   implies   Y’. We will
use both formulations interchangeably.
If a proposition is true, then is its converse always true?

Let us consider some examples of propositions and their converses,
Example 1:
• Proposition P: If it rains, then the road is wet.
• Converse Q: If the road is wet, then it has rained.
Proposition P is true but its converse, Q, may not be true. For instance,
the road may be wet because a tanker spilled water on the road!
A situation that illustrates why a given proposition is not necessarily
true is called a   counterexample  . It refers to an example that contradicts
the stated proposition.

Example 3:   In this example,   n is any positive integer.
• Proposition P: If   n is a perfect square, then it has an odd number
of factors.
• Converse Q: If   n has an odd number of factors, then it is a perfect
square.
You may recall that we came across these statements in the previous
grade. Which of them are true?
Discussion
Consider the following argument.
Each factor of a number has a ‘partner’ factor such that their product
yields the given number, e.g., 5 is a factor of 35, and 5 × 7 = 35. Here, 7 is the
partner factor of 5, and vice-versa. Let us focus on factor-partner pairs of
numbers, e.g., (1, 12), (2, 6), (3, 4) are the pairs for the number 12.
If a number has an odd number of factors, there must be a factor-partner
pair in which the same number repeats (e.g., the partner factor of 5 in 25).
If not, the given number will have an even number of factors since each
factor can be paired with its factor pair.
Thus, if a number has an odd number of factors, then it is a perfect
square.
What does this argument prove? Statement P or Q?

It is. The argument above can be modified to prove this.
We start with (iii). Clearly, (iii) implies (ii). Does (ii) imply (i)? Not

factors. For example, if the number of such pairs is two — say (  f, f) and

However, a number cannot have more than one factor pair with
repeating   factors.   Thus,   a square   number   necessarily   has an odd
number of factors. Hence Statement P is also true.
In this example, ‘  n is a perfect square’ and ‘  n has an odd number of
factors’ imply each other.

A Note
We have seen that ‘If X then Y’ can also be written as ‘X implies Y’.
These are not the only ways to express this meaning. It can be conveyed
through other words and sentence structures as well. For example, ‘Y
when X’ also gives the same meaning. The following two statements, for
instance, have the same meaning.
Proposition:   If a number is a perfect square, then it has an odd
number of factors.
Proposition:   A number has an odd number of factors when it is a
perfect square.

Chapter Summary
• A proposition   is a statement that is true or false.
• The   converse   of the proposition ‘if P then Q’ is the proposition
‘if Q then P’.
• It can happen that a proposition is true while its converse is false.
It can also happen that both the proposition and its converse are
true, or both are false.
• Counterexamples   are instances that demonstrate the falsity of
a given proposition.

What does the 100% stacked bar tell us?
In each garden, the total number of flowers for the whole year is
shown as one bar and is taken as 100%. The colours show what fraction
of that total bloomed in each season. So for Fatima’s garden, the bar tells
us: 50% of her flowers bloomed in summer, 30% in monsoon, and 20%
in winter. For Naveen’s garden, the bar tells us: 40% in summer, 35% in
monsoon, and 25% in winter.
Statement   (i): It compares   seasons   within   the same   garden
(Fatima’s). So, within Fatima’s garden, the summer part is the largest
fraction. This means she actually had more flowers in summer than in
monsoon. So, (i) must be true.
Statements (ii) and (iii):   These compare across gardens in the same
season.
For example, in statement (ii), Fatima has a higher percentage in
summer (50%) than Naveen (40%). But the chart does not tell us how
many flowers they each had in total. Naveen’s total could be much larger.
Then even 40% of Naveen’s total might still be more flowers than 50%
of Fatima’s total. That means we cannot be sure who has more flowers
in that season. Hence, (ii) and (iii) cannot be inferred from this graph.
Statement (iv):   A 100% stacked bar chart always shows each bar as
‘100%’ of that garden’s total. It does not tell us whether the totals for the
two gardens are equal or not. Many different total numbers can give the
same 100% bar. Therefore, we also cannot say anything about the total
over the whole year from this graph.
A 100% stacked bar chart helps us compare fractions/percentages
inside each garden, but it does not tell us the actual numbers of flowers.

The World of Algorithms
These days the word ‘algorithm’ pops up everywhere. What exactly is
an algorithm? What does it have to do with mathematics?
11.1 Adding Numbers
We first learned to add numbers by counting the
sum explicitly. To add 5 and 7, we would draw 5
dots to represent the number 5, then draw 7 more
dots to represent the number 7, and finally count
the dots, 12, to get the answer. Imagine adding
large numbers this way, for instance 473 + 695!
Fortunately, we discovered a much faster way to add numbers.
We wrote the numbers one below the other in the Indian number
system, with the rightmost digit aligned. We then added each column
from right to left, remembering to add a carry to the left if the total was
equal to or more than 10.
11.1.1 Adding Numbers Digit by Digit
This is an example of an   algorithm  , a step-by-step procedure to arrive
at an answer. We describe an algorithm by writing down the steps as
precisely as possible. For instance, here is one way to describe our
addition algorithm.
Algorithm to add two numbers
1. Write the numbers one below the other in the Indian base-
ten place-value   system   so that   the digits   are aligned
from right to left.
2. Add the rightmost digits.
– If the sum is less   than   10, write   the sum directly
below the two digits. Set the value of carry to 0.
– If the sum is more than 10, write the units digit of
the sum directly below the two digits. Set the value
of carry to 1.

Typically, any algorithm is built using such basic steps that we assume
we can perform directly.
The main advantage of this algorithm is that it is much faster than
counting explicitly. For instance, if we move from adding 3-digit numbers
to adding 4-digit numbers, we have 10 times as many dots to draw and
count. On the other hand, if we add the digits right to left, as in the
algorithm above, we only have to add 1 more column. Likewise, 5-digit
numbers would generate 100 times as many dots as 3-digit numbers,
but only 2 more columns to be added.

11.2 Greatest Common Divisor
Let us look at a familiar problem: computing the   greatest common
divisor (gcd) or highest common factor (hcf)   of two numbers. We
have already learned different ways to solve this problem, using prime
factorisation and other methods.
How can we solve the problem from the definition of gcd? How do
we write down an algorithm for this?
The first step is to understand what is required and break down the
problem into smaller pieces that we know how to solve.
• We want the greatest common divisor of two numbers.
• For this, we need to first compute the divisors of both numbers
and note them down.
• Once we have done this, we need to compare the two collections
of divisors and find the largest number that is present in both
collections.

11.2.1 Computing the Divisors of a Number
Suppose we want to compute   gcd(375, 825).
How do we find the divisors of 375? The divisors of a number are
just the factors of that number. Clearly any divisor, or factor, must lie
between 1 and 375. The simplest way to find the divisors is to check
each number between 1 and 375 and see if it divides 375.
We also have to keep track of the divisors of 375 so that we can
compare them with the divisors of 825. We can maintain each of these in
a sequence, or a   list. How do we create the list of divisors of a number?
When we start, we do not know any divisors, so the list of divisors is
initially empty. Every time we find a new divisor, we add it at the end
of the list.
Here is how we can write down this algorithm to find the list of
divisors of any number   n.
Algorithm to   find the divisors of n
1. Start with an empty list-of-divisors.
2. For each   number   j in the sequence   1,2,3,...,n
– if j divides n  , add j to the list-of-divisors.
Notice how we use names   list-of-divisors   and   j to identify the
values that we keep track of in this algorithm. If we do not use   j to
identify the number we are checking next as a possible divisor, it would
be clumsy to describe what to do each time we find a divisor.
Running through an algorithm step by step is also called   executing
the algorithm.   Let us execute   this algorithm   for a small   number,
say 18. We use square brackets to denote a list of values — for instance,
[1, 4, 9, 16].
Let us see how this algorithm works for a small number, say 18.
• Initially   list-of-divisors   is empty [ ].
• 1 divides 18, so we update   list-of-divisors   to [1].
• 2 divides 18, so we update   list-of-divisors   to [1, 2].
• 3 divides 18, so we update   list-of-divisors   to [1, 2, 3].
• 4 does not divide 18, so we leave   list-of-divisors   unchanged as
[1, 2, 3].
• 5 does not divide 18, so we leave   list-of-divisors   unchanged as
[1, 2, 3].

11.3 Data Structures
When   we execute an algorithm, we compute intermediate quantities,
such as the list   divisors-of-m   and the list   divisors-of-n  . To keep track
of these values, we use names such as   divisors-of-m   and   divisors-
of-n  , respectively, so that we can refer to them later in the algorithm.
As we remarked earlier, assigning names to intermediate values is
important for writing down algorithms concisely.
Also, these intermediate values are typically more complicated than
just simple numbers. In this case, we maintain lists of numbers and, in
fact, lists that are arranged in ascending order. A   list is an example of
a data structure  . Data structures allow us to organise information in
a manner that helps make an algorithm more efficient. We will explore
this idea in higher grades.

Compute both lists of divisors together
In the algorithm above, we first check all numbers between 1 and   m to
find the divisors of   m. Then we start again with 1 and check all numbers
between 1 and   n to find the divisors of   n. This means we have to scan
m + n possible divisors.
We can combine these two scans into one. Let   max  (m, n) be the
maximum of   m and   n. We run through each   j from 1 to   max  (m, n).
• If j divides   m we add   j to the list of divisors of   m.
• If j divides   n we add   j to the list of divisors of   n.
This reduces the number of possible divisors to be checked from
m + n to max  (m, n).
Directly compute the list of common divisors
If we combine the scans, we can directly compute the list of common
divisors, instead of first computing the divisors of each number and
then combining the lists.
• For each number   j from 1 to   max  (m, n), if j divides both   m and
n, add   j to the list of common divisors.

• For each number   j from 1 to   min(m, n), if j divides both   m and

11.3.3 Euclid’s Subtraction Algorithm
An elegant solution to achieve this was written down by Euclid, in his
book   Elements  . Historians believe that other Greek mathematicians
knew of this algorithm before Euclid, but he was the first to put it down
in writing.
Reducing a problem to a simpler one
The original version of Euclid’s algorithm makes use of the following
observation. Let us assume that   m ≥ n. Then,  gcd(m, n) is the same as
gcd(n, m – n).
Why is this the case?
• Let d be a common divisor of   m and   n. Then we can write   m as ad
and   n as bd, where   a and   b are natural numbers. This means that
m – n = ad – bd   = (a – b  )d, so d is also a divisor of   m – n.
• On the other hand, if   d divides both   n and   m – n, then   n = xd and
m – n = yd for some natural numbers   x and   y. This means that
n + (m – n) = m = xd + yd = (x + y)d, so d also divides   m.
This is shown visually below. If we can ‘tile’   m and   n using blocks of
size d, we can also tile   m – n by blocks of the same size. In fact, Euclid
used a similar pictorial proof to justify his algorithm.

This observation tells us that any common divisor of   m and   n is also

the set of common divisors are the same in both cases, which means
that   gcd(m, n) = gcd(n, m – n).
Euclid’s algorithm reduces the problem of finding gcd(  m, n) to that
of finding   gcd(n, m – n). The second problem involves smaller numbers,
so it should be a simpler problem to solve.

How do we solve it? We apply the same strategy: reduce it to
gcd(x, y) where   x = min(  n, m – n) and   y is the difference between   n and
m – n.
When do we stop? Suppose we compute the gcd of a number with
itself, say   gcd(23, 23). The answer should clearly be 23. When we
reduce this we get   gcd(23, 0). In other words, if we reach   gcd(m, 0), the
answer is   m.
Euclid’s algorithm for   gcd(m,n)
1. If m < n, reverse the numbers and compute gcd(n,m)
2. If n = 0, report the answer as m
3. Otherwise, reduce the problem to compute gcd(n,m  -n)
Let us apply this algorithm to   gcd(375, 825), the problem that we saw
before.
• Since 375 < 825, Step 1 asks us to reverse the numbers and compute
gcd(825, 375).
• Since 825 – 375 = 450, Step 3 asks us to reduce the problem to
computing   gcd(375, 450).
• Since 375 < 450, Step 1 asks us to reverse the numbers and compute
gcd(450, 375).
• Step 3 asks us to reduce the problem to computing   gcd(375, 75).
• Step 3 asks us to reduce the problem to computing   gcd(75, 300).
• Since 75 < 300, Step 1 asks us to reverse the numbers and compute

• Since   n = 0, Step 2 tells us the answer is 75.

11.3.4 Āryabhaṭa’s Division Algorithm
In Indian   treatises   like Āryabhaṭa’s   Āryabhaṭīya   (499   CE), we see
the “long division” method for finding GCD using Indian place value
numerals. It makes an efficient use of repeated “division by remainder”
in place of repeated subtraction. For example, when one starts with 99
and subtracts 2 repeatedly, what remains, once one cannot subtract 2
anymore, is the remainder when 99 is divided by 2.
Hence, to improve the performance of Euclid’s algorithm, we can
change the reduction step: instead of reducing   gcd(m, n) to gcd(n, m – n),
we reduce it to   gcd(n, m   mod   n), where   m mod   n stands for the remainder
when   m is divided by   n. For example, 17 mod 5 is 2 and 33 mod 7 is 5.
We saw earlier that a divisor   d ‘tiles’ both   m and   n if and only if it
‘tiles’ both   n and   m – n. In a similar way, we can argue that   d ‘tiles’ m and
n if and only if it tiles   m and   m mod   n. This justifies reducing   gcd(m, n) to
gcd(n, m mod   n).
Here is the modified algorithm. Notice that the only change is in the
third statement. The stopping condition is the same as before.
1. If m < n, reverse the numbers and compute gcd(n,m)
2. If n = 0, report the answer as m
3. Otherwise, reduce the problem to computing gcd(n, m mod n)
Consider the earlier example   gcd(99, 2) that took a long time to
converge.   Here   is what   happens   when   we execute   the improved
algorithm.
• Step 3 asks us to reduce the problem to computing   gcd(2, 1).
• Step 3 asks us to reduce the problem to computing   gcd(1, 0).
• Since   n = 0, Step 2 tells us the answer is 1.
In other words, we needed only two reduction steps to get to the
final answer. Check that this would happen for any similar problem of
the form   gcd(2k + 1, 2).

A Pinch of History
The influential Persian mathematician Al-Khwārizmī (780–850 CE), a
scholar at the “House of Wisdom” in Baghdad, developed a great liking
for Indian mathematics. He learnt Sanskrit, and around 820 CE wrote a
detailed treatise expounding the Indian place value number system and
the Indian procedures for the four fundamental operations — addition,
subtraction, multiplication, and division. His original is lost, but its
12th-century Latin translation,   Liber Algorismi de numero Indorum
(“The Book of Al-Khwārizmī on Indian Numerals”), survives and played
a major role in spreading Indian arithmetic through medieval Europe.
Al-Khwārizmī’s name became so closely tied to these methods that the
word algorismus — from Algoritmi, the Latin form of his name — came to
mean any Indian method for numerical computation; it later contracted
to algorism   and   then   algorithm.   Etymologically,   then,   “algorithm”
originally denoted an Indian method of arithmetic, before it broadened
to mean any well-defined, step-by-step systematic procedure for solving
a class of problems.

Chapter Summary
• An algorithm   is a systematic procedure to solve a problem.
• Algorithms consist of basic steps that we know how to execute.
• We may execute the same step a number of times; for instance, for
each   j in 1, 2, ...,   n, check if   j is a divisor of   n.
• A step in an algorithm may be conditional; for instance, add   j to
the list of divisors of   n only if   j divides   n.
• When we describe an algorithm to solve a problem, we need to
justify that it is correct.
• We can also analyse how many steps an algorithm takes, in terms
of the size of its input, and decide how efficient it is.

As we studied in Grade 8, parallelograms are quadrilaterals whose

13.2   Solution of Linear Equation in Two Variables
In earlier grades, we have seen how to solve linear equations in one
variable. What can we say about the solution of a linear equation
involving two variables?
13.2.1 Algebraic Meaning of Solution
A solution   of a linear equation in two variables is a pair of values of   x
and   y which satisfy the given equation.
Let us consider the equation 3  x + 2y = 12.
Here,   x = 2 and   y = 3 is a solution because substituting the values of   x
and   y into the given equation satisfies the equation.
Note that, 3  x + 2y = (3 × 2) + (2   × 3) = 12.
A solution can be written compactly as a pair of numbers. For example,
the solution   x = 2 and   y = 3 can be written as (2, 3). The order of the
numbers in this pair matters: the first number is the x-value, and the
second number is the y-value. Hence we call this an   ordered pair  .
Similarly, (4, 0) is also a solution of the equation 3  x + 2y = 12 since
substituting   x = 4 and   y = 0 leads to 3  x + 2y = (3 × 4) + (2   × 0) = 12.
What about the ordered pair (1, 3)?
We see that 3  x + 2y = 3 × 1 + 2 × 3 = 9   ≠ 12. So, (1, 3) is not a solution.

A Pinch of History
Solving systems of linear equations is one of the oldest problems in
mathematics, going back thousands of years. The earliest traces appear
in Babylonian cuneiform tablets (c. 2000 BCE). They were studied
systematically in ancient China; the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical
Art (compiled roughly two thousand years ago) devotes an entire
chapter, fangcheng, to systems of linear equations, solved by arranging
coefficients in columns of counting rods and then combining columns
to cancel out variables — a procedure essentially identical to modern
elimination,   fully   eighteen   centuries   before   Gaussʼs   name   became
attached   to it in Europe.   Many   Indian   mathematicians,   including
Āryabhaṭa (499 CE) and Brahmagupta (628 CE), also studied general
systems of equations in several unknowns using their own systematic
reduction techniques including elimination.
